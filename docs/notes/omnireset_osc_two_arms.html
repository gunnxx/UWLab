Two Arms, One Controller

The UR5e and the G1-D read the same kind of action and run the same control law, yet the G1-D hand darts where the UR5e hand glides. This short book explains why, measures the difference, and proposes a remedy.

Chapter 1

What the policy commands

Every tenth of a second the policy writes six numbers. The first three ask the hand to move along the x, y and z axes of the robot base, and the last three ask it to turn about those same axes. The policy never names a joint angle or a torque. It names a small change of the hand pose, and a controller underneath turns that wish into motor torques.

The six numbers first pass through a scale. Both robots use the same one, 20 millimetres per unit for each translation and 0.2 radians per unit for the turn about z. The controller then builds a target pose by adding the scaled change to the pose the hand has right now.

xtarget=xmeasured+s⊙a

Here a is the policy output and s is the scale. Two details matter for the rest of the book. The target is rebuilt from the measured pose at every policy step, so the controller only ever chases a point one action ahead of the hand. And nothing limits the size of a. A policy that outputs 5 asks for 100 millimetres just as easily as one that outputs 1 asks for 20.

Between two policy steps the physics engine advances twelve times, at 120 hertz. At each of those twelve substeps the controller measures the arm again and recomputes the torques, while the target stays fixed.

Chapter 2

A spring, a damper and a Jacobian

The controller behaves like an invisible spring stretched between the hand and the target, with a shock absorber mounted beside it. The spring pulls the hand toward the target with a force that grows with the distance. The shock absorber, which engineers call a damper, pushes against the hand with a force that grows with its speed. Written out for the force F on the hand, the law reads as follows.

F=Kpe−Kdx˙

The vector e is the pose error, the gap between target and hand, and x˙ is the hand velocity. The stiffness Kp sets how hard the spring pulls, and the damping Kd sets how hard the shock absorber resists motion.

A force at the hand is not something a motor can produce directly. The Jacobian J, a matrix that relates joint speeds to hand speed, translates the force into one torque per joint. The controller also adds a small posture term for the seven-jointed G1-D, which keeps the elbow near a preferred angle without moving the hand, and the actuator adds its own joint damping D. The full torque on the joints is therefore

τ=JT(Kpe−KdJq˙)+τposture−Dq˙

where q˙ collects the joint speeds. The torques are then clipped to each motor's limit. Notice what the law leaves out. It never accounts for how heavy the arm is. A heavy arm and a light arm receive the same force for the same error, and they respond very differently to it. That omission is the root of this whole story.

The configuration files do not store Kd directly. They store a number called the damping ratio, written ζ, and convert it with the rule below.

Kd=2ζKp

This rule is exact only for a hand that weighs one kilogram. Chapter 4 shows what it means for real arms.

Chapter 4

One mass on a spring

A whole arm is complicated, but along any single direction it behaves much like one mass. That mass is called the task-space inertia and is written Λ. It answers a simple question. If you push the hand with one newton, how quickly does it start to move? Physics gives the answer through the joint inertia matrix M and the Jacobian.

Λ=(JM−1JT)−1

With that single mass in hand, the controller reduces to the textbook mass on a spring with a damper.

Λx¨=Kp(xtarget−x)−Kdx˙

Two numbers describe how such a system moves. The natural frequency tells how quickly it reacts, and the true damping ratio tells whether it overshoots.

ωn=KpΛζtrue=Kd2KpΛ

For the UR5e moving along y, with a mass of 6.3 kilograms, the natural frequency is 5.6 radians per second and the true damping ratio is 1.2. The setting of 3 in the configuration file therefore produces a hand that is a little more than critically damped, which is exactly the calm, no-overshoot motion the UR5e shows. For the G1-D, with a mass of 1.2 kilograms, the same spring gives a natural frequency of 13 radians per second, more than twice as fast, and the controller adds no damping at all.

The speed the hand settles at

Because the target is rebuilt from the measured pose at every policy step, a policy that holds the same action keeps the spring stretched by the same amount, step after step. The hand then stops accelerating once the damper pushes back as hard as the spring pulls. Setting the two forces equal gives the cruising speed.

vcruise=KpsaKd

For the UR5e with an action of one, this is 200 times 0.02 divided by 84.9, or 47 millimetres per second. Notice that the hand mass has dropped out. Once the damper is present, the cruising speed depends only on the gains, the scale and the action. Without a damper the formula breaks down, and the only thing left to stop the G1-D hand is the faint joint damping in its actuator.

The cruising speed grows in direct proportion to the action a. Keep this in mind for Chapter 7, because the policy is free to choose a.

Chapter 6

Why the G1-D lost its damper

The missing damper was a deliberate choice. A comment in the G1-D configuration records that explicit damping in the controller made the light wrist unstable. Figure 4 confirms that warning, and a short calculation explains it.

The controller computes its damping torque from the velocity it measured at the start of a physics step, then holds that torque for the whole step of 1/120 of a second. Consider a single joint with inertia I spinning at speed v. After one step the damping torque has changed the speed to

vk+1=(1−KdΔtI)vk

If the bracket lies between minus one and one, the speed shrinks every step and the joint settles. If the damping is too strong for the inertia, the bracket drops below minus one, and the joint reverses direction faster than it was moving before. Each step then makes the motion larger. The stability condition is therefore

KdΔtI<2

The rotational damping of the UR5e recipe, 3.46 newton metres per radian per second, acts almost directly on the last wrist joint. Figure 5 compares the ratio for each wrist joint of the two robots.

Translational damping is a different matter. A force at the hand reaches the wrist joints through short lever arms, so the damping that the wrist feels from it is small. The measurements agree. The G1-D with the full UR5e translational damping ran smoothly in every direction, with no sign of chatter.

The actuator joint damping D also escapes the problem. The physics engine solves it as part of the motion rather than applying it as a fixed torque, which keeps it stable at any value. That is why the original author moved the G1-D damping there, and it is also why that location suits the wrist rotation.

Chapter 7

The policy pushes back

So far the action was held at one. A trained policy is under no such restraint. Neither robot clips its actions, and both policies have learned to command far more than one. The table below shows the size of the actions that three trained policies produced during 160 steps of peg insertion in 32 environments.

UR5e peg, full scale2.8 to 4.36.4 to 1923 to 69 %0.29 and 0.56 m/s
G1-D peg, full scale1.5 to 4.32.7 to 9.218 to 90 %0.30 and 0.94 m/s
G1-D peg, quarter scale5.0 to 9.811 to 1334 to 68 %0.26 and 0.55 m/s

The last row is the important one. When the action scale was cut to a quarter, the policy simply learned to output larger actions, about two to four times larger than before. The quarter-scale policy now moves its hand at nearly the same speeds as the UR5e policy. Its fastest moments dropped from 0.94 to 0.55 metres per second, but its typical fast motion barely changed.

This explains why the quarter-scale robot still looked too fast. A policy optimizes for reward, and reaching the peg quickly earns reward. If the controller makes each unit of action smaller, the policy asks for more units. The cruising speed formula of Chapter 4 makes the trade explicit. Any change to the stiffness, the scale or the damping is undone by a matching change in a, as long as a is free.

The UR5e policy is no gentler by nature. Its hand reaches 0.56 metres per second in its fastest moments, close to what its 1.57 radian per second joint limit allows. It looks calm partly because its damper keeps every motion smooth and partly because it is a large robot, so the same speed looks slower on it.

Chapter 8

A recipe for a calmer G1-D

Damping is the right instinct, and it belongs in the recipe. It makes the G1-D respond like the UR5e for each unit of action, and it removes the snap at the start and end of each motion. On its own, however, it will not slow the trained robot down, because the policy will learn to push harder. A calmer G1-D needs three changes together, followed by retraining.

  1. Add translational damping in the controller. Set the translational damping ratio to 3, as on the UR5e, and leave the rotational ratio at 0. The measurements show this is stable on the G1-D and that it brings the cruising speed down to the UR5e value of about 43 millimetres per second for each unit of action.

  2. Damp the wrist rotation in the actuator. Raise the joint damping of the three wrist joints from 1 to about 2 newton metres per radian per second, and keep it at 1 for the shoulder and elbow. The physics engine handles this damping implicitly, so it stays stable. In the test, the turn about z slowed from 34 to 14 degrees per second, close to the UR5e.

  3. Bound the action. Clip the policy output so that the speed formula has a ceiling the policy cannot negotiate away. With a clip at c, the fastest the hand can cruise becomes

    vmax=KpscKd≈0.047cm/s

    at the full scale with the damping of step 1. A clip of 1 gives 47 millimetres per second, which would make a 30 centimetre reach take more than six seconds of a sixteen second episode. A clip of 4 gives about 0.19 metres per second. The clip should be chosen from the speed you want, and the same reasoning sets the rotational ceiling. Chapter 9 derives a value from a speed that was actually watched.

Two cautions apply. First, the action clip reshapes what the policy can learn, so it calls for a fresh training run rather than a resumed one, and the episode length should be checked against the slower ceiling. Second, the reset-state datasets were recorded with the old controller. They describe poses rather than motions, so they remain valid, but the grasp hold test during recording uses the controller too and may give slightly different yields.

A cheaper check is possible before committing to a full run. Train for a few hundred iterations with each candidate clip, then measure the hand speed percentiles as in Chapter 7. The 99th percentile is the number that best matches the impression of a robot that moves too fast.

Chapter 9

Old policies on the new controller

Before retraining anything, the two full-scale G1-D policies were run with the controller of Chapter 8. The peg policy at iteration 2200 and the cube policy at iteration 1700 were both trained with the old controller. They received the translational damping ratio of 3 and the wrist joint damping of 2, but no action clip, and nothing in the networks changed. The episodes started from the same reset states as the rollouts in the gallery, so each episode can be compared with its counterpart.

The drop is expected. Each policy learned how far the hand travels for a given action, and the new controller moves the hand about five times less for the same action along y and z. A policy that expects the hand to arrive in two steps now finds it a fifth of the way there. The peg policy copes better than the cube policy, perhaps because insertion already rewards slow, careful motion near the hole.

The fastest moments, the 99th percentile, fell by almost two thirds, from about 0.9 to 0.32 metres per second, and the fastest turns fell by more than half. Those fast moments are what the eye notices, so this is the change that made the robot look calmer.

The last column shows something important. Even without retraining, the policies already push harder. The cube policy roughly tripled its typical action, and the share of its translation actions above one rose from about 9 to about 24 percent. Nothing in the network changed. The policy reacts to what it sees, and a hand that lags behind leaves a larger error in view, so the policy answers with a larger action. This is the closed-loop version of the effect in Chapter 7, and retraining would only strengthen it.

Keeping this speed after retraining

A policy retrained on the new controller will learn to use larger actions on purpose, and the speed would creep back up. The action clip of Chapter 8 prevents that. To keep the speed that was watched here, the cruising ceiling should sit near the fastest moments observed, about 0.32 metres per second. With the damping of Chapter 8 at full scale, each unit of action buys about 0.047 metres per second of cruising speed, so

c≈0.320.047≈7

The rotation agrees. With wrist damping of 2, one unit of action turns the hand at about 14 degrees, or 0.24 radians, per second about z, and a clip of 7 allows about 1.7 radians per second, close to the 1.4 to 1.5 observed. A clip of 7 therefore preserves the motion you saw while removing the room to speed up again.

Three limits apply to this estimate. The cruising speeds come from one arm pose, and the forward x direction was much slower there. A clip bounds the steady speed, not the brief surge at the start of a motion, so short peaks a little above the ceiling remain possible. And the success rates above belong to policies that were never trained on this controller, so they say nothing about what a retrained policy will reach. The two rollouts that finished are in logs/episode_renders/g1d_peg_newctl_s0, g1d_peg_newctl_s1 and g1d_cube_newctl.
