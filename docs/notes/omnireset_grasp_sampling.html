How the OmniReset grasp sampler places a hand on an object

A step by step walk through the antipodal sampler as it was, the reasons it fell short for the Dex3 hand and the big peg, and the three changes that followed: the mesh at its spawn scale, a pinch point beside the approach line, and a convention of the Dex3's own that lays a yawed axis along the pinch line. Every symbol in the formulas is tied to the variable that holds it in mdp/events.py.

1. What the sampler is for

The reset-state datasets of OmniReset need grasps that are known to hold. The grasp-sampling task produces them. It spawns a floating hand and one object in each of thousands of environments, places the open hand at a candidate pose around the object, closes it, lets physics settle, pokes the object a little, and finally asks whether the object is still in the hand. The poses that pass are written to Grasps/<object>/grasps.pt as the pose of the gripper body expressed in the object frame, together with the finger joint positions at that moment.

The part this page explains is the first box of that pipeline, the one that decides where the open hand is placed. It lives in the event term grasp_sampling_event in mdp/events.py. It computes a list of candidate poses once, when the first reset happens, and then every reset draws one candidate per environment at random from that list.

2. The frames and the numbers each hand brings

Two frames matter. The object frame \(O\) is attached to the object's root prim. For the peg its origin is at the centre of the block and \(z\) points along the long axis, so a standing peg has \(z\) pointing up. Every candidate pose is expressed in this frame, which is why a recorded grasp stays valid wherever the object later lies. The gripper frame \(G\) is the frame of the rigid body that the sampler moves. For the Robotiq it is the base link, for the Dex1 the gripper base and for the Dex3 the palm link right_hand_palm_link. A candidate is the homogeneous transform

\[ {}^{O}\mathbf{T}_{G} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{R} & \mathbf{t} \\ \mathbf{0}^\top & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \]

which maps a point written in the gripper frame to the same point written in the object frame. In the code one candidate is a 4 by 4 NumPy array, and the whole list is stacked into the tensor self.grasp_candidates.

The sampler does not know what a hand looks like. It reads a handful of numbers from the metadata.yaml next to the hand's USD file, through utils.read_metadata_from_usd_directory. These are the numbers and the symbols this page uses for them.

Besides the metadata, the event takes a few knobs from its configuration in config/ur5e_robotiq_2f85/grasp_sampling_cfg.py. The budget \(N_c\) is num_candidates and is one million by default. The number of spins \(N_o\) is num_orientations, sixteen by default. The number of standoff distances \(N_s\) is num_standoff_samples, thirty two by default. The lateral jitter \(\sigma_\ell\) is lateral_sigma and is zero by default, and the top-bias share \(\beta\) is top_bias_fraction, one by default, one half for the Dex1 and zero for the Dex3. Three knobs more belong to section 7: standoffs_within_clearance, off by default, and grasp_align_axis and orientation_sample_axis, which override the metadata's \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{o}}\) when set and are None by default.

3. The original sampler, one step at a time

before Until these changes there was only one way to make candidates, the antipodal sampler in _sample_antipodal_grasps. It is still there and it is still the fallback. The idea is old and simple. Pick a point on the surface, shoot a ray straight into the object, and wherever the ray comes out the other side you have a pair of opposite points that two fingers could squeeze between. Here is the procedure in the order the code runs it.

  1. Decide how many surface points to try. Every surface point will later be expanded into \(N_o \times N_s\) poses, so the number of surface points that fits the budget is \[ N_{\text{surf}} = \left\lfloor \frac{N_c}{N_o\,N_s} \right\rfloor, \] which is the local num_surface_samples. With the defaults that is \(10^6 / (16 \times 32) = 1953\) points.
  2. Sample the surface with a bias toward the top. The mesh is sampled ten times over, \(10\,N_{\text{surf}}\) points \(\mathbf{x}_i\) with the normals \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_i\) of the faces they were drawn from, by mesh.sample. Each point gets a score with two parts, \[ \text{score}_i = \underbrace{\frac{z_i - z_{\min}}{z_{\max} - z_{\min}}}_{\text{high for points near the top}} \;+\; \underbrace{\max(n_{z,i},\,0)}_{\text{high for faces that look up}}, \] the array top_bias_scores. Here \(z_i\) is the height of the point in the object frame, \(z_{\min}\) and \(z_{\max}\) are the lowest and highest of the \(10\,N_{\text{surf}}\) sampled heights, and \(n_{z,i}\) is the \(z\) component of the face normal. The first part says where the point is, the second which way the surface faces there, so a point on the top face of the standing peg scores close to two while a point on a side face just below the top edge scores close to one. Up means the object's own \(z\), which is up only because the scene spawns the object at the identity orientation. The \(\lceil \beta N_{\text{surf}} \rceil\) best scoring points are kept, and the remaining \(N_{\text{surf}} - \lceil \beta N_{\text{surf}} \rceil\) are drawn uniformly from the rest. With \(\beta = 1\) every kept point is near the top of the object. The Dex3 configuration uses \(\beta = 0.5\) so that a lying peg, which can only be held across its diameter from above, also gets side points.
  3. Find the opposite point. From each kept point a ray is cast inward along \(-\hat{\mathbf{n}}_i\) with mesh.ray.intersects_location. Of all the hits, the furthest one that is still within the aperture is taken, \[ \mathbf{y}_i = \arg\max_{\mathbf{h}\in\text{hits}_i}\; \lVert \mathbf{h} - \mathbf{x}_i \rVert \quad\text{subject to}\quad \lVert \mathbf{h} - \mathbf{x}_i \rVert \le w, \] the local opposing_point. If no hit is within \(w\) the point is dropped.

    This test checks the wrong length. It compares the aperture with how far the ray travels through the object. For the hands in this repository the hand later approaches along that same ray, so the length being checked is how deep the object is in front of the palm, not how wide it is between the fingers. On the standing big peg a ray from the top travels 12 cm, more than the 9 cm aperture, and the top approach is thrown away even though the 6 cm wide peg fits between the fingers.

    Section 4 shows the consequence in a diagram.

    The grasp axis and its length are \[ \ell_i = \lVert \mathbf{y}_i - \mathbf{x}_i \rVert, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{u}}_i = \frac{\mathbf{y}_i - \mathbf{x}_i}{\ell_i}, \] the locals axis_length and grasp_axis.
  4. Place the grasp centre on that axis. With the default lateral_sigma of zero, \(\rho_i\) is one half, so the grasp centre is the midpoint between the surface point and the opposite hit. For a point on the top face of a standing peg that puts the centre halfway down the peg, inside the solid. When \(\sigma_\ell > 0\) the centre slides along the axis by a truncated normal draw \(\rho_i\) that stays between the two surface points, \[ \mathbf{c}_i = \mathbf{x}_i + \rho_i\,\ell_i\,\hat{\mathbf{u}}_i, \qquad \rho_i \sim \mathcal{N}_{[0,1]}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2},\, \tfrac{\sigma_\ell}{\ell_i}\right), \] and \(\rho_i = \tfrac{1}{2}\) when \(\sigma_\ell = 0\). These are the rows of centers.
  5. Orient the hand. The hand's alignment axis \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\) is rotated onto the grasp axis \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_i\) by the smallest rotation that does so, which trimesh.geometry.align_vectors computes and the code calls align, \[ \mathbf{R}_{\text{align},i} = \operatorname{Align}(\hat{\mathbf{g}} \to \hat{\mathbf{u}}_i). \] That fixes one axis of the hand and leaves it free to spin about that axis. The spin is enumerated, \[ \theta_k = -\pi + \frac{2\pi k}{N_o - 1}, \quad k = 0,\dots,N_o - 1, \qquad \mathbf{R}_{\text{spin}}(\theta_k) = \operatorname{Rot}(\hat{\mathbf{o}},\,\theta_k), \] the array spins, built from rotation_angles. Both \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{o}}\) come from the metadata unless the configuration overrides them, which only the Dex3's does, see section 7.
  6. Enumerate how far back the hand stands. The standoff \(s\) is the distance from the body origin to the pinch point along the approach. It starts at the finger offset, where the fingertips would close exactly on the centre, and grows until the hand is an object diagonal plus half the clearance further away, \[ s_j \in \operatorname{linspace}\!\left(f,\; f + d + \tfrac{c_f}{2},\; N_s\right), \qquad d = \lVert \text{mesh.extents} \rVert, \] the tensor standoff_distances. Here \(d\) is the diagonal of the object's bounding box, mesh_diagonal, about 14.7 cm for the big peg. The upper bound is only a generous limit that grows with the object. It has no derivation behind it, and the larger standoffs are mostly wasted, but they cost nothing to try. A second sweep exists for a hand that holds the object in a pocket rather than along its approach: with standoffs_within_clearance the standoffs run over \([f - c_f/2,\; f + c_f/2]\) instead. It is off by default, and section 7 says why the Dex3 turns it on.
  7. Compose the pose. For every combination of point, spin and standoff one transform is built, \[ {}^{O}\mathbf{T}_{G} = \operatorname{Trans}(\mathbf{c}_i)\;\mathbf{R}_{\text{align},i}\;\mathbf{R}_{\text{spin}}(\theta_k)\;\operatorname{Trans}(-\,s_j\,\hat{\mathbf{a}}). \] Read it from the right. The hand frame is first pulled back by \(s_j\) along its own approach axis, so that the point \(s_j\hat{\mathbf{a}}\) of the hand sits at the origin. It is then spun, aligned and finally carried to the grasp centre. The point of the hand that ends up at \(\mathbf{c}_i\) is therefore \(s_j\hat{\mathbf{a}}\), which for \(s_j = f\) is the pinch point of a hand whose pinch point is on the approach line. In the code the four factors are the arrays centers, align, spins and pullbacks, and two einsum calls combine them for every point, spin and standoff at once into transforms, shape \((N, 4, 4)\), ordered by point, then spin, then standoff.

    Example: the Dex1 on the big peg, from the top. Suppose the sampled point is on the top face, so the grasp centre is 6 cm below it, halfway down the peg. The standoff then decides how deep the peg sits between the fingers.

    StandoffFingertipsPad on the pegResult
    \(s = f\)at the centre, 6 cm below the top6 cm, the front of the base 6 mm above the topdeepest possible hold
    \(s = f + 3\,\text{cm}\)3 cm below the top3 cma hold
    \(s = f + 5.4\,\text{cm}\)6 mm below the top6 mmthe shallowest hold
    \(s > f + 6\,\text{cm}\)above the top facenonethe fingers close on air

    Pulling the hand back by \(s - f\) therefore moves the fingertips up the peg, and the window of useful standoffs is the top 6 cm. In the antipodal sampler this top point never reaches step 7 on the big peg, since step 3 rejects it, so the big peg can only be grasped from the side.

Keep one picture in mind. Since \(\hat{\mathbf{a}}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\) are the same vector for the Robotiq and the Dex1, aligning \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\) with the grasp axis means the hand approaches along the line through the two contact points. The direction in which the fingers actually close is then whatever the spin \(\theta_k\) happens to turn it to. The aperture test of step 3 is therefore not a test of how wide the fingers must open. It is a test of how deep the object is along the approach.

4. Three things the original sampler could not do

Each of these is dealt with by one of the changes that follow. The mesh size is fixed in section 5 and the fingertip offset in section 6. The top approach is not handled: the faulty test is still in the sampler, and the Dex3 avoids it by grasping the big peg from the side with its squeeze turned across the pinch line, which section 7 explains.

It could not approach a tall object from above

This one is best described as a bug. A correct antipodal sampler checks the width the fingers have to open to, which is the extent of the object across the direction the fingers close. The test in step 3 instead checks the length of the ray, and because step 5 aligns the approach with that ray, the ray length is the depth of the object in front of the palm. The two lengths agree only when the object is as deep as it is wide. On a tall object approached from above they differ by a lot, and the sampler rejects grasps that the hand could easily make.

Take the big peg standing on the table. It is 6 by 6 by 12 cm. A surface point on its top face has an upward normal, so the inward ray goes straight down through the whole block and comes out 12 cm later at the bottom face. The test \(\ell \le w\) compares 12 cm with the 9 cm aperture of the Dex3 and rejects the point. Every top point fails in the same way, so the sampler offered no approach from above at all. Points on the sides pass, since the ray crosses only 6 cm there, and they give horizontal approaches. For the standing G1 the horizontal approach is the awkward one. The right arm reaches the peg most comfortably coming down on it at about 45 degrees, and that pose simply is not in the candidate set.

It put the Dex3's fingertips beside the object

Step 7 places the point \(s\hat{\mathbf{a}}\) of the hand at the grasp centre. For a hand whose pinch point lies on the approach line that is the right point. For the Dex3 the pinch point is \(\mathbf{p} = (0.098, 0.072, 0)\) in the palm frame while \(s\hat{\mathbf{a}}\) at the first standoff is \((0, 0.072, 0)\). The difference is \(\mathbf{p}_\perp\), 9.8 cm along the palm's \(x\) axis. The original composition therefore put the palm normal through the grasp centre and the thumb and fingertips 9.8 cm away from it, closing on air.

It grasped the mesh at the wrong size

The big peg is not a new USD file. It is the peg USD spawned with a scale of two. The sampler extracts the mesh from the USD prim in _extract_mesh_from_asset, and the points it read were the authored ones at 3 by 3 by 6 cm. Every centre and every standoff was computed for a block half the size of the one in the simulation.

5. Change one: the mesh takes the spawn scale

after This is the smallest change. After the mesh is converted to a trimesh, the spawn scale \(\mathbf{k}\) of the object asset is applied to its vertices,

\[ \mathbf{v}' = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{k})\,\mathbf{v}, \qquad \mathbf{k} = \texttt{asset.cfg.spawn.scale}, \]

through mesh.apply_scale, and the resulting extents are logged so that a wrong size shows up in the console. For the big peg the log line reads object mesh extents (m) [0.06, 0.06, 0.12]. The dataset folder also carries the scale now, Grasps/Peg_x2 instead of Grasps/Peg, through utils.object_dataset_name, so grasps of the two sizes never mix.

6. Change two: a pinch point beside the approach line

after The fix for the Dex3's fingertips is to pull the hand back by the whole pinch point, not just by its component along the approach. The pinch point at standoff \(s\) is

\[ \mathbf{p}(s) = s\,\hat{\mathbf{a}} + \mathbf{p}_\perp, \]

and the last factor of the composition in step 7 becomes

\[ {}^{O}\mathbf{T}_{G} = \operatorname{Trans}(\mathbf{c}_i)\;\mathbf{R}_{\text{align},i}\;\mathbf{R}_{\text{spin}}(\theta_k)\;\operatorname{Trans}\!\big(-\,\mathbf{p}(s_j)\big). \]

In the code the array pullbacks holds -(standoff * approach + self.pinch_lateral_offset) for every standoff. Written out without the homogeneous matrices, with \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{R}_{\text{align}}\mathbf{R}_{\text{spin}}\), the position of the gripper body is

\[ \mathbf{t} = \mathbf{c} - \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{p}(s), \]

which says exactly what one wants: rotate the pinch point into the object frame and step back from the centre by that much. For the Dex1 and the Robotiq \(\mathbf{p}_\perp = \mathbf{0}\) and the formula is the old one, so their candidates do not move.

7. Change three: the Dex3 lays a yawed axis along the pinch line

after The first problem of section 4 is not fixed in the sampler. It is sidestepped by the Dex3, with the same code and a convention of its own. Every candidate the sampler makes comes from a pinch line through the object, and for the standing big peg the only lines that pass the test of step 3 run across the peg from side to side. A hand that approaches along such a line comes in horizontally, which the standing G1 reaches awkwardly. The Dex3 therefore does not approach along the line at all. Its configuration names an axis 45 degrees from the squeeze axis in the palm plane, \(\hat{\mathbf{g}} = \hat{\mathbf{o}} = (0.71, -0.71, 0)\), and the sampler lays that axis along the pinch line and spins the hand about it. The squeeze then crosses the line at 45 degrees, which on the square peg means across the diagonal of its cross-section, with the corners in the pocket between the thumb and the fingertips. The spin sweeps the whole hand around the line with that yaw kept, so the palm comes from above, from the side, or anywhere between, and the 45 degree tilt that the arm reaches is among the candidates. The convention was found in the inspector on the big peg.

Four parameters of config/g1_dex3/grasp_sampling_cfg.py carry it, all of them read by the code of section 3 without a branch of their own. grasp_align_axis and orientation_sample_axis override the metadata's \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{o}}\) with the yawed vector. standoffs_within_clearance replaces the standoff sweep of step 6 with one over the pocket's depth. top_bias_fraction is zero, since with the squeeze across the line every side face of a standing peg yields useful lines and a top point only yields a line down the whole height. The spin and standoff counts stay the Robotiq's. The Robotiq and Dex1 configurations set none of these, so their candidates are what they always were.

Where each hand sets its axes

The two axes come from the hand's metadata unless the configuration overrides them. The Dex3's metadata names its squeeze axis \(+x\) for both, so that even without the configuration a Dex3 candidate is a squeeze across the ray, and the configuration then turns that by 45 degrees. When the two axes are not parallel the event logs the angle between them at start-up, since such a spin turns \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\) off the line, which the next subsection explains. The spin is a rotation about the hand's own axis

Look at where \(\mathbf{R}_{\text{spin}}\) stands in the product: to the right of the alignment. A rotation on the right of a frame's rotation acts in that frame's own coordinates. Hence \(\mathbf{R}_{\text{spin}}(\theta)\) turns the hand about its own \(\hat{\mathbf{o}}\). When \(\hat{\mathbf{o}} = \hat{\mathbf{g}}\), the alignment has just put that axis on the pinch line, so the same rotation can be written on the other side,

\[ \mathbf{R}_{\text{align}}\,\operatorname{Rot}(\hat{\mathbf{g}}, \theta) \;=\; \operatorname{Rot}(\hat{\mathbf{u}}, \theta)\,\mathbf{R}_{\text{align}}, \]

a turn about the pinch line in the object frame by the same angle. The two descriptions are the same motion: "the hand rotated by \(\theta\) about its own \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\)" and "the whole hand swung by \(\theta\) around the line through the object" are one candidate. For the Dex3 the squeeze axis is 45 degrees from \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\), so it rides around the pinch line on a cone of half-angle 45 degrees, always crossing the line at the centre.

If \(\hat{\mathbf{o}}\) were a different axis, say the squeeze axis itself, the spin would keep that axis fixed in the world and swing \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\) off the pinch line. That is a legitimate family too, a roll of the hand about its squeeze, but a different one, and it is not what the Dex3 configuration does. The event logs the angle between the two axes whenever they differ, so the choice is never silent.

The hand-tuned grasp was found by aligning the palm normal with \(-z\) and rolling the hand 45 degrees about the object's \(x\). In that pose the hand's \(x\) coincided with the object's \(x\), so the roll was about the hand's own squeeze axis. The yaw and the roll are told apart below.

Yaw, roll, and the hand-tuned grasp

Two angles describe the Dex3's pose relative to a pinch line, and it helps to keep them apart.

  • The yaw is the angle between the squeeze axis and the pinch line, in the palm plane. It is fixed by \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\): 45 degrees in the configuration, which on the square peg takes the diagonal, 8.5 cm, instead of the 6 cm face to face. This is what makes the hold firm, since the peg's corners sit in the pocket between the thumb and the two fingertips instead of its flat faces. It was found by inspection and is the one number worth tuning.
  • The roll is the spin \(\theta\), the position of the palm around the pinch line. Negative angles bring the palm in from above, \(\theta = -45^\circ\) being the tilt that the standing arm can reach. The grid of sixteen angles brackets it with \(-48^\circ\) and \(-24^\circ\).

The hand-tuned grasp had roll \(-45^\circ\) and yaw zero: its squeeze was across the peg's faces. The sampler's candidates near \(\theta = -45^\circ\) are therefore the same tilt with the squeeze turned onto the diagonal, not the identical pose. If you ever want yaw zero back, set both axes to \((1, 0, 0)\) in the configuration and the squeeze lies on the line again.

What the width test means now

Step 3 of section 3 compares the pinch line's length with the aperture. For the Robotiq and the Dex1, whose approach is on the line, \(\ell\) is the depth of the object in front of the palm, and the test is kept as it was on purpose. For the Dex3 the line is the face-to-face width of the object, while the fingers actually span the width at 45 degrees to it, \(\sqrt{2}\) longer on a square section. The test is therefore optimistic by that factor: 6 cm passes, the real 8.5 cm would pass too, but an 8 cm square would pass the test and not fit. The physics test after the hand closes catches such cases, so the width test is a cheap filter rather than the last word.

The standoff sweep

The standoff \(s\) is the distance from the body origin to the point placed on the centre, along the approach. At \(s = f\) the pinch point is on the centre. The legacy sweep, which the Robotiq and Dex1 keep, runs from \(f\) outward by an object diagonal plus half the clearance, and suits a hand that approaches along the pinch line and can hold the object anywhere along it. For the Dex3 the approach is across the line, so pulling back moves the centre out of the pocket, and what matters is only how deep in the pocket the object sits. With standoffs_within_clearance the sweep runs over the finger clearance centred on \(f\), that is 5.7 to 8.7 cm from the palm for the Dex3, against a pocket that holds the centre between 5.0 and 8.5 cm. With the Robotiq configuration's 32 standoffs they sit about a millimetre apart.

The same pinch line for the Dex1

The Dex1 and the Robotiq run through exactly the same seven steps of section 3, and the contrast with the Dex3 is easiest to see on the same pinch line. For them the axis on the line is the approach, so the hand comes in along the ray, from the sampled point's side, with its pinch point at the centre inside the object. The fingers, which slide along \(\pm y_G\), then close across the ray, and the spin \(\theta\) is a wrist roll that chooses in which direction across the object they close. For the Dex3 the hand sits beside the ray with the squeeze crossing it, and the spin chooses which side the palm comes from. The figure draws the Dex1 on the pinch line of the worked example above, in the same view as the Dex3 figure.

What is deliberately left as it is

  • The spin range. Half of the spins put the palm below the object, where the table is. Restricting \(\theta\) to a range, or filtering spins by the direction of the palm normal in the object frame, would drop them before the physics test does. It would be a range on the existing grid, not a change of axis.
  • The width test above stays optimistic by \(\sqrt{2}\) for the Dex3.
  • The Robotiq and Dex1 keep the approach on the pinch line and the legacy standoff sweep. Giving them a convention of their own would be the same handful of parameters in their configurations, and would change their candidate sets, so it is not done.

The Dex3 grasps recorded before this change were made under the earlier convention, on a big peg that then weighed 150 g in the grasp-sampling task. The big peg is authored at 200 g now and the Dex3 candidates are a different set, so the Dex3 grasp file and everything recorded from it need recording again. The Robotiq and Dex1 files are unaffected.

8. From a candidate to a recorded grasp

For completeness, this is what happens to a candidate once it exists. On every reset the event draws an index per environment uniformly from the candidate list, torch.randint(0, len(self.grasp_candidates), ...). It makes sure the hand is open and still, and then it composes the candidate with the object's current world pose,

\[ {}^{W}\mathbf{T}_{G} = {}^{W}\mathbf{T}_{O}\;{}^{O}\mathbf{T}_{G}, \]

in _apply_grasp_transforms_vectorized, using math_utils.combine_frame_transforms on positions and quaternions, and writes the result as the root pose of the floating hand. The object itself is placed by a separate event at a fixed height with gravity switched off, so the hand and the object start the episode hanging in space in the candidate's relative arrangement.

The binary gripper action then closes the hand. One second in, global_physics_control_event switches gravity on, and between one and two seconds it applies a small random force and torque to the object, which it now removes again once the window closes. At the last step of the four second episode check_grasp_success judges the result. The object must have stayed within 25 cm of where it started and above 25 cm height, it must have been still for five consecutive steps, no gripper joint may be moving abnormally, the hand's sampled surface points may not penetrate the object, and the closure joints must on average have travelled more than ten percent of the way from open to closed. When all of that holds, the recorder stores the pose of the gripper body in the object frame, recomputed from the final simulated poses rather than copied from the candidate, together with the hand's joint positions. Those are the grasps the reset-state tasks later read back.
