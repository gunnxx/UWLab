Two Simulators, One Controller: The Stage 1 and Stage 2 Training Recipe

Why the policy is first trained in an idealised simulator and then fine-tuned in a realistic one, what the implicit and explicit actuator models actually do with the controller's torque, how the success-gated curriculum moves the simulator from one to the other, and why the training and evaluation environments use different gains and action scales.

This note is a companion to the controller tutorial From a Desired End-Effector Pose to Joint Torques. That tutorial explains how the relative Cartesian operational-space controller (OSC) turns a policy action into joint torques. This note explains what happens to those torques afterwards, and how the training recipe is organised around that.

1The two stages at a glance

The OmniReset insertion policy is trained in two stages. Stage 1 trains the policy from scratch in a deliberately forgiving simulator. Stage 2 loads the converged stage 1 checkpoint and continues training while the simulator is gradually made to behave like the physical robot. The controller, the reward, the observations and the network are identical in both stages. What changes is the plant that the controller acts on, together with the gains and the action scale that the controller is given. Figure 1 summarises the recipe.

2Where the torque goes: the actuator layer

Every physics step, the controller computes a torque vector for the arm joints and hands it to the articulation as an effort target. That target does not reach the physics engine directly. In Isaac Lab an actuator model sits between the action term and the simulator, and the actuator model decides what the simulator finally receives. The two training stages use two different actuator models, so this layer is the first thing to understand.

2.1 The joint drive that PhysX offers

PhysX can run a proportional-derivative (PD) drive on every joint. Given a position target \(q^\ast\), a velocity target \(\dot q^\ast\) and a feed-forward effort \(\tau_{\mathrm{ff}}\), the drive applies

\[ \tau \;=\; k_s\,(q^\ast - q)\;+\;k_d\,(\dot q^\ast - \dot q)\;+\;\tau_{\mathrm{ff}}, \]

where \(k_s\) is the joint stiffness and \(k_d\) the joint damping. The important property is how PhysX evaluates this expression. The solver treats the drive as a constraint and solves for the velocity at the end of the step, so the damping term is evaluated with the new velocity rather than the old one. This is an implicit integration, and an implicit spring-damper is stable for any positive gain and any step size. Section 2.5 shows why that matters.

2.2 The implicit actuator

Isaac Lab's ImplicitActuator is a thin wrapper around that PhysX drive. Its compute method receives the targets, writes some telemetry, and returns the targets unchanged. The stiffness and damping it carries are written into the PhysX drive once at start-up, and PhysX does the rest.

2.3 Explicit actuators

Every other actuator in Isaac Lab is explicit. The base class is IdealPDActuator. It evaluates the same PD expression itself, in Python, with the current joint state, clips the result to the actuator's own effort limit, and then does something decisive on lines 332 to 334: it moves the computed effort into the effort slot and erases the position and velocity targets. PhysX therefore receives a pure effort, held constant for one physics step, and its internal drive never engages.

Because the Python code can do anything to the targets before they reach PhysX, explicit actuators are where motor imperfections are modelled. DelayedPDActuator is the one used here. It draws a delay in physics steps for every environment at reset, between min_delay and max_delay, and pushes the incoming targets through a circular buffer of that length before evaluating the PD law. With the configuration used in OmniReset, the delay is zero or one physics step.

2.4 With zero gains, both actuators are a pipe

The OmniReset arm configurations set the actuator stiffness and damping to zero in both variants. With \(k_s = k_d = 0\), the PD expression above reduces to \(\tau = \tau_{\mathrm{ff}}\), and \(\tau_{\mathrm{ff}}\) is exactly the torque the OSC produced. Neither actuator is doing position control. The only closed loop in the system is the task-space PD law inside the OSC, and the actuator is the pipe through which its torque flows. Figure 2 shows the two pipes side by side.

The configurations below are the two variants for each robot. Note that the G1-D implicit actuator carries a joint damping of 1.0 N m s/rad while every other entry is zero. The reason is the subject of the next section.

Three small differences remain between the two pipes, and they are worth naming because they are the whole difference between the stage 1 plant and the stage 2 plant as far as the actuator is concerned.

  1. Delay. The explicit pipe holds each torque for zero or one physics step before it applies. At 120 Hz that is up to 8.3 ms, which is the order of the command latency of a real joint controller.
  2. Where the effort clamp lives. The implicit pipe clamps inside the solver with effort_limit_sim. The explicit pipe clamps in Python with effort_limit first, and the solver clamp still applies afterwards. The numbers are the same, so the effect is the same.
  3. Joint damping on the G1-D. The implicit G1-D arm has 1.0 N m s/rad of joint damping inside the PhysX drive. The explicit G1-D arm has none. That is a real difference in plant behaviour, and it is discussed in Section 7.

2.5 Implicit versus explicit damping, and the light wrist

Why does any of this matter if the gains are zero anyway? Because the OSC itself contains a damping term, \(K_d\,J\dot q\), and that term is evaluated explicitly: the controller computes it once from the current velocity and holds it for a whole physics step. Consider a single joint with inertia \(I\) about its axis and a damping torque \(-c\,\omega\) applied this way. Over one step of length \(h\) the velocity updates as

\[ \omega_{n+1} \;=\; \Big(1 - \frac{c\,h}{I}\Big)\,\omega_n \quad\text{(explicit)}, \qquad \omega_{n+1} \;=\; \frac{\omega_n}{1 + c\,h / I} \quad\text{(implicit)}. \]

The implicit update shrinks the velocity for every \(c > 0\). The explicit update shrinks it only while \(c\,h / I < 2\). Beyond that bound each step overshoots zero by more than it started with, and the velocity grows without limit. The bound is therefore

\[ c \;<\; \frac{2\,I}{h}. \]

The numbers for the G1-D wrist are unforgiving. The wrist links weigh about 85 g, with an inertia of roughly \(5\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) about the roll axis, and the physics step is \(h = 1/120\,\mathrm{s}\). The bound is then about 0.12 N m s/rad. The rotational task-space damping of the OSC is \(K_d = 2\zeta\sqrt{K_p}\), which is about 3.5 N m s/rad with the UR5e training gains and about 14 N m s/rad with the evaluation gains. When the wrist roll axis is aligned with the task rotation axis, the transposed Jacobian maps that task damping almost one to one onto the wrist joint. Both values exceed the bound by more than an order of magnitude.

What the code says

The comment in the G1-D implicit actuator configuration records the empirical version of this argument: an explicit task-space damping torque of a few N m s/rad at 120 Hz overshoots the velocity each substep and diverges within a handful of substeps, verified with probe scripts on 26 October 2026. The isolated-link bound above is a simplification. The effective inertia seen by a joint includes everything downstream of it, and PhysX substeps the solve, so the real threshold is higher than 0.12. The order of magnitude is what matters, and it explains why the UR5e, whose wrist links are about ten times heavier and carry a 0.5 kg gripper, tolerates the same damping that destabilises the G1-D.

The G1-D configuration therefore makes two linked choices. The OSC damping ratio is set to zero on all six axes, so the controller applies no explicit damping at all. The damping is moved into the implicit actuator as a joint damping of 1.0 N m s/rad, which PhysX integrates implicitly and which is stable regardless of its value. The gains are otherwise identical to the UR5e recipe.

3Stage 1: learning the task

Stage 1 exists to learn the task, not to match the hardware. The simulator is kept as clean as the task allows, so that the policy receives a consistent learning signal while it discovers how to grasp, align and insert. The configuration is the base environment with the training events and the training action set. On the UR5e the robot is the implicit variant throughout the scene definition. On the G1-D the scene also selects the implicit variant, and the environment classes are one-line specialisations of the UR5e ones.

3.1 What is randomised, and what is not

The training events are the shared base events plus the reset-state sampler. The base events randomise the things that belong to the task: the friction of the robot, the peg, the hole and the table, the masses of the same bodies, and the gripper actuator gains. They deliberately exclude the things that belong to the motor: the arm's joint friction, armature and delay, and the OSC gains. The docstring of the base event class says so explicitly, and the training event class only adds the reset sampler.

The action set is the soft one. The translational stiffness is 200 N/m, the rotational stiffness 3 N m/rad, and one unit of policy output moves the target by 2 cm or by 0.02 rad about x and y and 0.2 rad about z. The UR5e uses a damping ratio of 3 on translation, which is strongly overdamped, and 1 on rotation. The G1-D uses zero for the reason given in Section 2.5.

4Stage 2: moving the policy toward the real robot

A policy that has converged in stage 1 has never felt joint friction, has never waited for a delayed torque, and has only ever pushed with soft gains and large steps. Each of these is different on the hardware. Stage 2 closes the gap by continuing training while the simulator is moved, a little at a time, from the stage 1 plant to the real one. It is a fine-tuning stage because it starts from the stage 1 checkpoint rather than from random weights.

4.1 The configuration

The fine-tune environment differs from the training environment in three declarations. The events are the fine-tune events, which add two randomisation terms. The curriculum is no longer empty. And after the base initialisation, the scene's robot is replaced by the explicit variant, so the delayed actuator is in place from the first step of stage 2.

4.2 The progress variable and what it controls

Everything that stage 2 changes about the simulator is driven by a scalar progress variable \(p \in [0, 1]\). At \(p = 0\) the simulator is the stage 1 simulator with the explicit actuator. At \(p = 1\) it is the evaluation simulator. The progress variable is stored on the event terms and read every time they run, which is at every environment reset. Three families of quantities depend on it.

Arm dynamics from system identification. The calibrated armature \(a^{\mathrm{id}}_j\), static friction \(\mu^{\mathrm{id}}_{s,j}\), dynamic-to-static ratio \(\rho^{\mathrm{id}}_j\) and viscous friction \(b^{\mathrm{id}}_j\) of each joint \(j\) are read from the robot's metadata file. At each reset of an environment, a fresh scale \(s \sim U(0.8, 1.2)\) is drawn per joint and per quantity, and the values written into PhysX are

\[ \begin{aligned} a_j &= (1-p)\,a^{0}_j + p\,s\,a^{\mathrm{id}}_j, \\ \mu_{s,j} &= p\,s\,\mu^{\mathrm{id}}_{s,j}, \qquad \mu_{d,j} = \min\!\big(s\,\rho^{\mathrm{id}}_j\,\mu_{s,j},\;\mu_{s,j}\big), \qquad b_j = p\,s\,b^{\mathrm{id}}_j, \\ d &\sim U\{\,0,\dots,\mathrm{round}(p\,d_{\max})\,\}, \quad d_{\max} = 1, \end{aligned} \]

where \(a^{0}_j\) is the armature the asset started with and \(d\) is the motor delay in physics steps. Friction starts at exactly zero and the delay starts at exactly zero, so the \(p = 0\) plant is frictionless and instantaneous, as in stage 1.

4.3 The update rule

The progress variable is not advanced on a timer. It is advanced by the policy's own success rate, so the simulator only becomes harder when the policy has shown that it can cope with the current difficulty. The rule is a simple hysteresis controller. Let \(\bar s\) be the mean success rate across the reset-state distributions, as reported by the reset sampler's success monitor. Every 200 environment steps,

\[ p \;\leftarrow\; \begin{cases} \min(1,\; p + 0.01) & \text{if } \bar s > 0.95, \\ \max(0,\; p - 0.01) & \text{if } \bar s < 0.90, \\ p & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \]

The band between 0.90 and 0.95 is where the policy is allowed to recover without the difficulty changing. A separate warm-up gate holds \(p = 0\) until the success rate has reached 0.95 at least once, so that a freshly loaded checkpoint which performs poorly under the new actuator is given time before anything else changes. Once opened, the gate stays open.

The timings follow from the numbers in the rule. The counter is the environment's global step counter, which advances once per policy step regardless of how many parallel environments there are. Reaching \(p = 1\) from 0 needs at least 100 raises, hence at least 20,000 policy steps. At 32 policy steps per PPO iteration, that is at least 625 iterations, or 2,000 s of simulated time per environment. Any excursion below 0.90 adds to this, because every retreat has to be climbed again.

4.4 Why ramp rather than switch

One might ask why the plant is not simply switched to its final form at the start of stage 2. The reason is that the stage 1 policy is tuned to the stage 1 plant. Under stiff gains and real friction the same actions produce different motion, the observations drift out of the distribution the policy has seen, and the success rate collapses. PPO can recover from a collapse, but it does so slowly and sometimes to a worse policy. The ramp keeps the policy inside a regime where it mostly succeeds, so every update is made from informative, mostly successful rollouts. The success gate turns the ramp speed into a measured quantity rather than a guess.

6Why training and evaluation differ

Table 1 showed three controller parameters that differ between the training and evaluation action sets: the stiffness, the damping ratio and the action scale. None of them is a separate design. The evaluation values are the end of the stage 2 ramp, and the training values are its start. The question is therefore why the ramp exists at all, parameter by parameter.

6.1 Stiffness: friction sets a floor on the useful gain

Consider the controller holding the end effector against a resisting force. At rest the task-space PD law reduces to \(F = K_p\,e\), so a resisting force \(F_f\) that the controller must overcome leaves a steady-state error

\[ e_{\mathrm{ss}} \;=\; \frac{F_f}{K_p}. \]

On the real arm, joint friction and the stiction of a peg in a hole both act as such a force. Suppose the friction of the wrist joints is equivalent to 5 N at the end effector, which is an illustrative figure rather than a measurement. At \(K_p = 200\,\mathrm{N/m}\) the controller stops 25 mm short of its target. At \(K_p = 1000\,\mathrm{N/m}\) it stops 5 mm short. For a peg with a fraction of a millimetre of clearance, the first number makes the task impossible and the second makes it merely hard. The stiff gain is the one that the real robot's controller runs, chosen against the friction found by system identification. The soft gain exists only because it is easier to learn under, as Section 6.3 explains.

6.2 Damping ratio: overdamped for exploration, critical for deployment

The UR5e trains with a translational damping ratio of 3, which is strongly overdamped. An overdamped loop approaches its target slowly and never overshoots, so a random early policy cannot make the arm swing through the table or the hole. The evaluation ratio of 1 is critically damped, which is the fastest response without overshoot, and is what the hardware controller uses. The G1-D trains with no explicit damping at all, for the stability reason in Section 2.5, which makes its jump from training to evaluation damping larger than the UR5e's.

6.3 Action scale: how far the arm moves per policy step

With soft gains the controller only ever reaches a fraction of a 2 cm step within the 0.1 s policy period, so the effective motion is gentle even though the commanded step is large. With stiff gains the step is reached within the period (see Figure 3 of the controller tutorial), and the step size becomes the speed:

\[ v \;=\; \frac{\sigma}{\Delta t_{\mathrm{policy}}} \;=\; \begin{cases} 0.2\,\mathrm{m/s} & \text{at } \sigma = 2\,\mathrm{cm},\\ 0.1\,\mathrm{m/s} & \text{at } \sigma = 1\,\mathrm{cm},\\ 0.02\,\mathrm{m/s} & \text{at } \sigma = 2\,\mathrm{mm}. \end{cases} \]

Shrinking the scale as the gains stiffen keeps the end effector speed in a range that insertion tolerates, and the 2 mm vertical scale gives the policy millimetre control over the one axis that needs it. The alternative of clipping the pose error inside the controller would saturate the PD law and flatten the relationship between action and motion. Scaling the action instead keeps that relationship linear, which is what a policy gradient needs.

6.4 Why not train stiff from the start

Put the three together and the ordering is forced. A stiff controller with a 2 cm step applies a 20 N task force to every step, against 4 N for the soft one. An untrained policy emits random 2 cm steps ten times a second. Stiff gains therefore mean hard contacts, frequent collisions and many episodes that end in a termination before any reward is collected. Soft gains make exploration survivable. Once the policy knows what to do, the gains can be raised and the steps shortened, and the policy adapts to the changed response because it observes the end-effector pose and velocity rather than its own commands.

A lower success rate than the plain evaluation is expected, and the gap is a measure of how much a stage 2 would change rather than a defect.
  2. The explicit G1-D actuator has no joint damping. The implicit variant carries the 1.0 N m s/rad that replaces the OSC's explicit damping. The explicit variant, which a stage 2 would use, sets damping to zero, and the terminal damping ratio of 1 would re-introduce the explicit task-space damping of about 14 N m s/rad that Section 2.5 argues is unstable on this wrist.
  3. There is no system identification for the G1-D. The sysid event reads an armature, static_friction, dynamic_ratio and viscous_friction block from the robot's metadata file. The G1-D metadata file has no such block, so the UR5e fine-tune events cannot be instantiated against it as they stand.
