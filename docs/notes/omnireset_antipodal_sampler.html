The antipodal sampler, with a yawed axis on the pinch line

How the OmniReset grasp sampler turns an object mesh into candidate hand poses, as the code stands now: the same antipodal sampler for every hand, with the Dex3's convention set by three vectors and two knobs in its configuration, and where the 45 degree tilt of the hand-tuned grasp lives in it. The page stands on its own. Where it describes something that differs from the earlier sampler, the one documented in How the grasp sampler places a hand on an object, it says so with a mark like this: changed. Section 2 collects every such mark in one table. Every symbol is tied to the variable that holds it in mdp/events.py.

1. Where the antipodal sampler sits

The grasp-sampling task is one floating hand and one object, with no arm and no table. Its reset event, grasp_sampling_event in mdp/events.py, builds a list of candidate hand poses the first time it runs and draws from that list at every reset afterwards. Each candidate is the pose of the gripper body in the object frame, \({}^{O}\mathbf{T}_{G}\), so it does not depend on where the object happens to be. The list comes from one of three sources, tried in a fixed order in _generate_grasp_candidates:

SourceRuns whenWhat it produces
Fixed graspfixed_grasp.enabled is trueOne pose from a hand-written spec: a center in the object frame, an approach direction the hand's approach axis is aligned with, extra rpy_deg about the object's axes, and an optional standoff. For the inspector.
Nominal graspsnominal_grasps is non-empty\(N_c\) random perturbations of such specs, bounded by an angle, a centre spread and a standoff range.
Antipodal samplerotherwise\(N_c\) poses from the object mesh. The rest of this page describes it.

The two hand-written sources are described in sections 7 and 8 of the earlier page and have not changed. The Robotiq and the Dex1 have always used the antipodal sampler.

changed The Dex3 now uses the antipodal sampler too. Its configuration used to list two hand-written grasps, one from above and one from the side, and the nominal sampler scattered candidates around them. That list is empty now, so the Dex3 falls through to the antipodal branch like the other hands. The two grasps remain in the file as constants, one of them as the inspector's default fixed grasp, and can be put back into the list to return to the scatter.

What the sampler reads

Three things go in. The first is the object mesh, extracted from the spawned asset at its spawn scale, so a peg spawned at twice the size is sampled at twice the size. The second is the hand's metadata.yaml, which gives the approach direction, the alignment and spin axes of section 4, the pinch depth \(f\) along the approach, the finger clearance \(c_f\), the aperture \(w\), and the pinch point, whose part perpendicular to the approach is \(\mathbf{p}_\perp\). The third is the set of event parameters in the task configuration, every one of which can be overridden for a run with env.events.grasp_sampling.params.<name>=<value>: the budget \(N_c\), the spins \(N_o\) and standoffs \(N_s\) per surface point, the top bias \(\beta\), the centre spread \(\sigma_\ell\), and the standoff knob this page introduces. Section 12 lists them all with their variables.

What happens to a candidate

At a reset the event draws one index per environment being reset, uniformly from the list. It makes sure the hand is open and still, composes the candidate with the object's current world pose, \({}^{W}\mathbf{T}_{G} = {}^{W}\mathbf{T}_{O}\,{}^{O}\mathbf{T}_{G}\), and writes the result as the root pose of the floating hand. The object itself is held by another event at a fixed height with gravity off, so hand and object start the episode hanging in space in the candidate's arrangement. The binary hand action then closes the hand. One second in, gravity is switched on, and between one and two seconds a small random force and torque are applied to the object. At the end of the four second episode check_grasp_success judges the hold: the object must have stayed within 25 cm of where it started and above 25 cm height, it must have been still for five consecutive steps, no hand joint may be moving abnormally, the hand may not penetrate the object, and the closure joints must on average have travelled more than ten percent of the way from open to closed. For every success the recorder stores the hand's pose in the object frame, recomputed from the final simulated poses, together with the hand's joint positions. Those recorded grasps are what the reset-state tasks later read back.

Two consequences matter for reading the rest. The sampler's own tests are cheap filters, and the physics test is the one that decides. The candidate list is also built once per run, so a parameter that changes it, such as the axes of section 4, takes effect at start-up and not between resets.

changed One thing in this flow is new. An empty candidate list, which happens when the object is wider than the aperture everywhere or the surface budget is too small, now stops at the first reset with a message that says so, instead of an obscure error from the random draw.

3. The idea in one paragraph

An antipodal grasp is two opposite contact points that a pair of fingers can squeeze between. The sampler finds such pairs by casting a ray into the object from a surface point, and the segment from the point to the far hit is the pinch line. It then has to decide which axis of the hand to lay along that line and which axis to spin the hand about. For the Robotiq and the Dex1 both are the approach, so the hand comes in along the pinch line and the spin is a wrist roll. That works because those grippers are symmetric about their approach. For the Dex3 the configuration names an axis 45 degrees from the squeeze axis in the palm plane, and spins about that same axis. The squeeze then crosses the pinch line at 45 degrees, which on a square peg means across the diagonal of its cross-section, and the spin sweeps the whole hand around the pinch line with that yaw kept, so the palm comes from above, beside, or anywhere between. The code path is the same for all three hands. Only parameters differ.

unchanged The Robotiq and Dex1 metadata and configurations were not touched, so their candidate sets are exactly what they were. The candidate transforms are now built in one vectorised pass instead of a Python loop, checked to reproduce the old loop's numbers to float32 rounding, and every new parameter defaults to the old behaviour.

4. Three hand axes and where each hand sets them

The sampler uses three unit vectors in the gripper body frame. Each gripper's metadata.yaml provides them, and the grasp-sampling event's parameters can override the second and third per configuration or per run, which is how the Dex3 sets its convention without rebuilding its asset.

changed Before, the two axes came from the metadata and from nowhere else, and the Dex3's metadata named its approach \(+y\) for both, the same convention as the Robotiq and the Dex1. Now the parameters grasp_align_axis and orientation_sample_axis override the metadata when they are set, the Dex3's metadata names its squeeze axis \(+x\) for both, and its task configuration overrides both with the yawed vector. The Robotiq and Dex1 configurations leave both parameters at None, so for them nothing moved. When the two axes are not parallel the event logs the angle between them at start-up, so the choice is never silent.

The same pinch line for the Dex1

The Dex1 and the Robotiq run through exactly the same seven steps, and the contrast with the Dex3 is easiest to see on the same pinch line. For them the axis on the line is the approach, so the hand comes in along the ray, from the sampled point's side, with its pinch point at the centre inside the object. The fingers, which slide along \(\pm y_G\), then close across the ray, and the spin \(\theta\) is a wrist roll that chooses in which direction across the object they close. For the Dex3 the hand sits beside the ray with the squeeze crossing it, and the spin chooses which side the palm comes from. 5. The sampler, step by step

This is _sample_antipodal_grasps in the order it runs. Steps 1 to 4 do not look at any hand axis, and their code computes what it always did; where the text quotes the Dex3's numbers, those are parameter values and not new mechanics. Steps 5 to 7 are where the axes and the two knobs enter, and where the code changed.

  1. Decide how many surface points to try. Every surface point becomes \(N_o \times N_s\) poses, so the surface budget is \(N_{\text{surf}} = \lfloor N_c / (N_o N_s) \rfloor\). Every hand uses the Robotiq configuration's \(N_o = 16\) and \(N_s = 32\), so a million candidates means about 1 950 surface points.

    unchanged The formula, the code and the counts are the same for every hand.

  2. Sample the surface. The mesh is sampled ten times over and a subset is kept. The share \(\beta\) of that subset comes from the top-scored points, high and facing up, and the rest is uniform over the surface. The Dex3 configuration sets \(\beta = 0\): every face of a standing peg yields pinch lines across the peg, and the top bias only served hands that approach along the ray. On the standing big peg uniform sampling puts about nine points in ten on the four side faces, by area.

    unchanged The sampling and the top bias are what they were. The Dex3's \(\beta = 0\) is a value, inherited as 1 before and unused while the scatter ran.

  3. Find the opposite point. From each kept point \(\mathbf{x}_i\) a ray is cast inward along \(-\hat{\mathbf{n}}_i\). Of the hits, the furthest one within the aperture is the opposite point \(\mathbf{y}_i\), \[ \ell_i = \lVert \mathbf{y}_i - \mathbf{x}_i \rVert \le w, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{u}}_i = \frac{\mathbf{y}_i - \mathbf{x}_i}{\ell_i}. \] The segment from \(\mathbf{x}_i\) to \(\mathbf{y}_i\) is the pinch line, \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_i\) its direction and \(\ell_i\) its length. Section 9 says what the test against \(w\) means under each convention.

    unchanged The ray cast and the test against \(w\) are exactly what they were.

  4. Place the centre on the line. With \(\sigma_\ell = 0\) the centre \(\mathbf{c}_i\) is the midpoint of the pinch line. For a side point of a peg that is a point on the peg's axis at the height of the sampled point, which is also why heights come for free: the surface points lie at every height.

    unchanged The midpoint, or the truncated-normal draw along the line when \(\sigma_\ell > 0\), as before.

  5. Align. The hand's alignment axis is rotated onto the pinch line by the smallest rotation that does so, trimesh.geometry.align_vectors, \[ \mathbf{R}_{\text{align},i} = \operatorname{Align}(\hat{\mathbf{g}} \to \hat{\mathbf{u}}_i). \] For the Dex3 this puts the yawed axis on the line, so the squeeze axis crosses the line at 45 degrees through the centre, and the thumb and fingertips land on the object where that crossing line leaves it. On a square peg sampled from a face, that is corner to corner.

    changed The rotation is the same, the smallest one that carries \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\) onto the line. What changed is which axis \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\) is: before, always the metadata's, which for every hand was the approach; now the configuration's when it sets one. For the Robotiq and Dex1 that is still the approach. For the Dex3 it is the yawed vector of section 4 instead of its approach, which is the whole change of convention in one line.

  6. Spin. One axis is fixed and the hand is free to turn about it. That turn is enumerated, \[ \theta_k = -\pi + \frac{2\pi k}{N_o - 1}, \quad k = 0, \dots, N_o - 1, \qquad \mathbf{R}_{\text{spin}}(\theta_k) = \operatorname{Rot}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}, \theta_k). \] The grid is closed, so its first and last angles are the same pose. With \(N_o = 16\) the step is 24 degrees and there are fifteen distinct spins. Section 6 is about what this rotation is, and section 7 about what each angle looks like.

    changed The grid is the same closed grid as before, and the rotation is about \(\hat{\mathbf{o}}\) in the hand frame as before. As with \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\), the axis can now come from the configuration. For the Dex3 that is the yawed vector, equal to \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\), so the spin sweeps the hand around the pinch line. The count of spins is the Robotiq's.

  7. Pull back and compose. The standoffs \(s_j\) are either the legacy sweep from \(f\) outward by an object diagonal, or, with standoffs_within_clearance, \(N_s\) values over \([f - c_f/2,\; f + c_f/2]\), which is what the Dex3 uses; a single standoff is \(f\) in both cases. For every point, spin and standoff one transform is built, \[ {}^{O}\mathbf{T}_{G} = \operatorname{Trans}(\mathbf{c}_i)\;\mathbf{R}_{\text{align},i}\;\mathbf{R}_{\text{spin}}(\theta_k)\;\operatorname{Trans}(-\,s_j\,\hat{\mathbf{a}} - \mathbf{p}_\perp). \] Read from the right: the hand is moved so that its point \(s_j\hat{\mathbf{a}} + \mathbf{p}_\perp\) sits at the origin, spun, aligned, and carried to the centre. For \(s_j = f\) that point is the pinch point, so the pinch point lands on \(\mathbf{c}_i\). In the code the four factors are centers, align, spins and pullbacks, combined for all candidates at once by two einsum calls, and the result is one array of shape \((N, 4, 4)\) ordered by point, then spin, then standoff.

    new The sweep over \([f - c_f/2,\; f + c_f/2]\) is new and off by default; the legacy sweep is untouched and is still what the Robotiq and Dex1 get. changed The product itself used to be built in a Python loop over points, spins and standoffs. It is now one vectorised pass, two einsum calls over the four factors, returning a float32 array of shape \((N, 4, 4)\) in the same order as the loop did, point, then spin, then standoff. The numbers agree with the old loop to float32 rounding; the vectorisation changes no candidate.

6. The spin is a rotation about the hand's own axis

Look at where \(\mathbf{R}_{\text{spin}}\) stands in the product: to the right of the alignment. A rotation on the right of a frame's rotation acts in that frame's own coordinates. Hence \(\mathbf{R}_{\text{spin}}(\theta)\) turns the hand about its own \(\hat{\mathbf{o}}\). When \(\hat{\mathbf{o}} = \hat{\mathbf{g}}\), the alignment has just put that axis on the pinch line, so the same rotation can be written on the other side,

\[ \mathbf{R}_{\text{align}}\,\operatorname{Rot}(\hat{\mathbf{g}}, \theta) \;=\; \operatorname{Rot}(\hat{\mathbf{u}}, \theta)\,\mathbf{R}_{\text{align}}, \]

a turn about the pinch line in the object frame by the same angle. The two descriptions are the same motion: "the hand rotated by \(\theta\) about its own \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\)" and "the whole hand swung by \(\theta\) around the line through the object" are one candidate. For the Dex3 the squeeze axis is 45 degrees from \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\), so it rides around the pinch line on a cone of half-angle 45 degrees, always crossing the line at the centre.

If \(\hat{\mathbf{o}}\) were a different axis, say the squeeze axis itself, the spin would keep that axis fixed in the world and swing \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\) off the pinch line. That is a legitimate family too, a roll of the hand about its squeeze, but a different one, and it is not what the Dex3 configuration does. The event logs the angle between the two axes whenever they differ, so the choice is never silent.

The hand-tuned grasp was written as rpy_deg = (45, 0, 0), a roll about the object's \(x\) after the palm normal had been aligned with \(-z\). In that pose the hand's \(x\) coincided with the object's \(x\), so the roll was about the hand's own squeeze axis. Section 8 relates that roll to the spin here.

8. Yaw, roll, and the hand-tuned grasp

Two angles describe the Dex3's pose relative to a pinch line, and it helps to keep them apart.

  • The yaw is the angle between the squeeze axis and the pinch line, in the palm plane. It is fixed by \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\): 45 degrees in the configuration, which on the square peg takes the diagonal, 8.5 cm, instead of the 6 cm face to face. This is what makes the hold firm, since the peg's corners sit in the pocket between the thumb and the two fingertips instead of its flat faces. It was found by inspection and is the one number worth tuning.
  • The roll is the spin \(\theta\), the position of the palm around the pinch line. Negative angles bring the palm in from above, \(\theta = -45^\circ\) being the tilt that the standing arm can reach. The grid of sixteen angles brackets it with \(-48^\circ\) and \(-24^\circ\).

The nominal top grasp of config/g1_dex3/grasp_sampling_cfg.py has roll \(-45^\circ\) and yaw zero: its squeeze was across the peg's faces. The sampler's candidates near \(\theta = -45^\circ\) are therefore the same tilt with the squeeze turned onto the diagonal, not the identical pose. If you ever want yaw zero back, set both axes to \((1, 0, 0)\) in the configuration and the squeeze lies on the line again.

9. What the width test means now

Step 3 compares the pinch line's length with the aperture. For the Robotiq and the Dex1, whose approach is on the line, \(\ell\) is the depth of the object in front of the palm, and the test is kept as it was on purpose. For the Dex3 the line is the face-to-face width of the object, while the fingers actually span the width at 45 degrees to it, \(\sqrt{2}\) longer on a square section. The test is therefore optimistic by that factor: 6 cm passes, the real 8.5 cm would pass too, but an 8 cm square would pass the test and not fit. The physics test after the hand closes catches such cases, so the width test is a cheap filter rather than the last word.

10. The standoff sweep

new The second sweep described here, standoffs_within_clearance, did not exist. The legacy sweep is unchanged and remains the default.

The standoff \(s\) is the distance from the body origin to the point placed on the centre, along the approach. At \(s = f\) the pinch point is on the centre. The legacy sweep, which the Robotiq and Dex1 keep, runs from \(f\) outward by an object diagonal plus half the clearance, and suits a hand that approaches along the pinch line and can hold the object anywhere along it. For the Dex3 the approach is across the line, so pulling back moves the centre out of the pocket, and what matters is only how deep in the pocket the object sits. With standoffs_within_clearance the sweep runs over the finger clearance centred on \(f\), that is 5.7 to 8.7 cm from the palm for the Dex3, against a pocket that holds the centre between 5.0 and 8.5 cm. With the Robotiq configuration's 32 standoffs they sit about a millimetre apart.

11. What is deliberately left as it is

  • The spin range. Half of the spins put the palm below the object, where the table is. Restricting \(\theta\) to a range, or filtering spins by the direction of the palm normal in the object frame, would drop them before the physics test does. It would be a range on the existing grid, not a change of axis.
  • The width test of section 9 stays optimistic by \(\sqrt{2}\) for the Dex3.
  • The nominal sampler and the fixed grasp are still there, with the same precedence as in section 1: fixed grasp, then nominal grasps when the list is non-empty, then antipodal. The Dex3 configuration now leaves the list empty, and the two hand-written grasps remain as constants for the inspector and as an alternative source.
  • The recording flow of section 1, from the drawn candidate to the physics test and the stored pose, is untouched.
  • The Robotiq and Dex1 keep the approach on the pinch line and the legacy standoff sweep. Giving them a convention of their own would be the same handful of parameters in their configurations, and would change their candidate sets, so it is not done.
